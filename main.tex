\begin{abstract}
This report studies the structure and decarbonization constraints of the French electricity system using hourly data for 2024. The data used in the calculations were originally sourced from the CentraleSupélec Edunao platform and made available by the course professors. The code, notebooks, figures, and report sources are available in the project repository at \url{https://github.com/baunilhamarga/tp-climat.git}. The first part analyses electricity consumption, including annual energy demand, peak and minimum load, per-capita indicators, daily, weekly, and seasonal load cycles, an order-of-magnitude estimate of electricity used for heating, and the additional annual demand associated with full electrification of the light-duty vehicle fleet. The second part examines the production mix, annual generation by technology, capacity factors, and the construction of net demand after subtracting must-run renewable production. The third part uses this net demand to size a simplified dispatchable generation fleet through screening curves, comparing nuclear, coal, gas, and oil technologies under fixed assumptions for investment, fuel cost, efficiency, and CO2 price. The resulting dispatch and cost estimates are compared with the installed French fleet and recorded production data. The analysis is intentionally simplified and does not model electricity markets, interconnections, detailed hydro management, storage operation, unit commitment, reserves, or network constraints. Its purpose is therefore not to reproduce the actual French power system, but to identify the main orders of magnitude and planning trade-offs involved in supplying residual electricity demand with a lower-carbon generation fleet.
\end{abstract}

\section*{Introduction}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Introduction}

This report follows the lab exercise on the decarbonization of an electricity generation fleet. The analysis is based on hourly French electricity data for 2024, originally sourced from the CentraleSupélec Edunao platform and made available by the course professors. The code, notebooks, figures, and report sources are available on GitHub.\footnote{\url{https://github.com/baunilhamarga/tp-climat.git}} The calculations use simplified power-system planning tools to connect demand, production, residual load, and generation costs. The aim is to identify the main orders of magnitude rather than to reproduce the full operation of the French electricity system. For this reason, the calculations neglect market mechanisms, interconnections, detailed operational constraints, and dynamic unit commitment effects. The results should therefore be read as a structured technical approximation of the planning problem.

\section{Exercise 1. Consumption analysis}

The input data for this section are the hourly values in \texttt{Donnees\_France\_2024\_fr\_cet.csv}. The load column is interpreted as an hourly average active power in megawatts. Since each entry represents one hour, the annual electrical energy is obtained by summing the hourly powers and expressing the result in megawatt-hours. Per-capita quantities use the metropolitan France population at 1 January 2024, equal to 66.380 million inhabitants according to INSEE.\footnote{\url{https://www.insee.fr/fr/statistiques/5225246}}

\subsection{Completeness of the hourly time series}

The dataset contains 8784 hourly values. This is exactly the number expected for 2024, since 2024 is a leap year and therefore contains
\[
366 \times 24 = 8784
\]
hours. No missing hourly record is detected at this level of aggregation.

\subsection{Maximum and minimum hourly consumption}

Table~\ref{tab:exercise1-extrema} gives the extrema of the hourly load series. The maximum occurs on 10 January at noon, during winter and during normal daytime activity. This is consistent with the strong temperature sensitivity of French electricity demand, in particular because of electric heating. The minimum occurs on 12 May at 06:00, during a spring Sunday morning. This period combines low economic activity, low lighting demand, and limited heating or cooling needs.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Extrema of the 2024 hourly load series.}
  \label{tab:exercise1-extrema}
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    Quantity & Power & Date and time \\
    \midrule
    Maximum hourly load & \SI{82.800}{\giga\watt} & 2024-01-10 12:00 CET \\
    Minimum hourly load & \SI{29.575}{\giga\watt} & 2024-05-12 06:00 CEST \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

\subsection{Annual average power}

The annual average consumed power is
\[
\overline{P} = \SI{48.917}{\giga\watt}.
\]
With a metropolitan population of 66.380 million inhabitants, the corresponding per-capita average power is
\[
\frac{\overline{P}}{N} =
\frac{\SI{48.917e9}{\watt}}{\num{66.380e6}}
= \SI{736.9}{\watt}
\]
per inhabitant. This value is an average electrical power over the full year, not an average total primary energy use.

\subsection{Annual electrical energy}

The annual electrical energy consumed is
\[
E = \sum_{h=1}^{8784} P_h \Delta t
= \SI{429.686}{\tera\watt\hour},
\]
with \(\Delta t = \SI{1}{\hour}\). The per-capita annual consumption is then
\[
\frac{E}{N}
= \SI{6473}{\kilo\watt\hour}
\]
per inhabitant per year.

\subsection{Temporal evolution and load cycles}

Figure~\ref{fig:exercise1-timeseries} shows the hourly load over the full year. The main variation scales are visible directly on the curve. The daily cycle has a typical mean peak-to-trough amplitude of about \SI{14.0}{\giga\watt}. It is driven by the day-night rhythm of residential and tertiary activity, with additional effects from cooking, lighting, and heating regulation. The weekly cycle produces an average weekday-weekend gap of about \SI{6.3}{\giga\watt}, mainly due to lower office and industrial activity during weekends. The seasonal cycle is the largest low-frequency component. The difference between the highest and lowest monthly mean loads is about \SI{22.6}{\giga\watt}, with winter demand much higher than summer demand because of electric heating.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/exercise_1-5.pdf}
  \caption{Hourly electricity load in France in 2024.}
  \label{fig:exercise1-timeseries}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Order-of-magnitude estimate of heating electricity}

The heating contribution cannot be measured directly from this file alone, since the load series does not separate end uses. A simple estimate is obtained by taking the mean load over June, July, and August as a non-heating baseline. This baseline is \SI{41.8}{\giga\watt}. Summing the positive hourly excess above this value gives
\[
E_{\mathrm{heating}} \simeq \SI{72.0}{\tera\watt\hour}.
\]
This corresponds to approximately
\[
\frac{E_{\mathrm{heating}}}{E} \simeq \SI{16.7}{\percent}
\]
of the annual electrical consumption. This result should be interpreted as an order of magnitude. It includes part of the winter-specific demand that is correlated with temperature, but it may also include other seasonal effects. Conversely, any summer cooling demand is included in the chosen baseline and is not separated.

\subsection{Load duration curve}

The load duration curve in Figure~\ref{fig:exercise1-duration} is obtained by sorting all hourly load values in decreasing order. It shows how long the power system must be able to supply a given load level. The left part corresponds to rare peak-load hours, reaching about \SI{83}{\giga\watt}. The central part is flatter and represents the majority of normal operating hours. The right part approaches \SI{30}{\giga\watt}, corresponding to low-load periods such as nights, weekends, and mild-weather days. This curve is useful for generation fleet sizing because it separates peak capacity needs from energy supplied over many hours.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/exercise_1-7.pdf}
  \caption{Load duration curve for the French 2024 hourly electricity load.}
  \label{fig:exercise1-duration}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Electricity demand for a fully electrified light-vehicle fleet}

For 40 million light vehicles, an annual distance of \SI{11700}{\kilo\metre} per vehicle, and a consumption of \SI{18}{\kilo\watt\hour} per \SI{100}{\kilo\metre}, the annual electricity demand is
\[
E_{\mathrm{EV}}
= 40 \times 10^6
\times 11700
\times \frac{18}{100}
= \SI{84.24e9}{\kilo\watt\hour}
= \SI{84.24}{\tera\watt\hour}.
\]
This represents about \SI{19.6}{\percent} of the 2024 electricity consumption. The value is an annual energy requirement only. The impact on peak demand would depend on charging profiles, charging power limits, and possible smart charging strategies.

\section{Exercise 2. Production analysis}

\subsection{Annual production by technology}

The production data are hourly average powers in megawatts. Annual electrical energy production by technology is calculated by summing the hourly values over the full year. Table~\ref{tab:exercise2-annual-prod} presents the annual energy produced by each technology and their share in the total production mix. The studied generation sources are onshore wind, solar photovoltaic, run-of-river hydro, natural gas, petroleum, and nuclear.

    \midrule
    Nuclear & 375.17 & 71.54 \\

Nuclear generation dominates the French production mix in 2024, accounting for approximately 71.5\% of the studied production sources. This reflects the long-standing role of nuclear power in the French electricity system. Run-of-river hydroelectric and onshore wind are the next largest contributors, together representing about 25\% of production. Natural gas and petroleum have lower annual output, reflecting their function as flexible or backup resources for peak demand and system balancing. Solar photovoltaic production, while growing, remains relatively modest in the total mix at this stage of deployment.

\subsection{Capacity factors}

The capacity factor is a key metric for comparing the efficiency of different generation technologies. It is defined as the ratio of actual annual energy production to the theoretical maximum energy that could be produced if the facility operated at full installed capacity for the entire year:

\[
f_c = \frac{E_{\mathrm{annual}}}{P_{\mathrm{installed}} \times 8784 \text{ hours}}.
\]

Table~\ref{tab:exercise2-capacity} presents the installed capacities, annual production, and capacity factors for each technology studied in France.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Annual production, installed capacity, and capacity factors by technology, France 2024.}
  \label{tab:exercise2-capacity}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Technology & Capacity (MW) & Annual energy (TWh) & Capacity factor (\%) \\
    \midrule
    Nuclear & 61175 & 375.17 & 70.49 \\
    Run-of-river hydro & 12896 & 72.17 & 63.87 \\
    Onshore wind & 20757 & 58.98 & 32.39 \\
    Gas & 12906 & 8.68 & 7.71 \\
    Solar PV & 16825 & 6.34 & 4.30 \\
    Oil & 4447 & 2.16 & 5.48 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Nuclear power exhibits the highest capacity factor at approximately 70.5\%, indicating that nuclear plants operate nearly continuously throughout the year except for planned maintenance and refueling outages. Run-of-river hydroelectric plants achieve a capacity factor of 63.9\%, which is relatively high because river flows are generally available year-round, although not fully controllable.

Wind and solar technologies show considerably lower capacity factors. Onshore wind achieves 32.4\%, limited by intermittent wind conditions throughout the year. Solar photovoltaic has the lowest capacity factor at 4.3\% due to the fundamental constraint of daylight availability and cloud cover. These lower factors are characteristic of renewable technologies and necessitate supporting capacity or storage in systems with high renewable penetration.

Natural gas and petroleum have the lowest capacity factors among the technologies studied, at 7.7\% and 5.5\% respectively. This reflects their role as flexible resources that operate primarily during peak demand periods and for system balancing, rather than as baseload generation.

  \centering

  \caption{Capacity factors by production technology in France, 2024. Nuclear and hydro achieve the highest utilization rates, while solar and petroleum operate infrequently.}
  \label{fig:exercise2-capacity-factors}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Net consumption time series}

In a real power system, the electricity demand must be met by generation sources that are either dispatchable or managed through storage and demand response. Variable renewable generation (wind and solar) cannot reliably meet demand on their own and must be considered as reducing the net demand that must be supplied by controllable sources. The net consumption is defined as:

\[
\text{net consumption} = \text{gross consumption} - \text{must-run production},
\]

where must-run production comprises the intermittent renewable sources: onshore wind, solar photovoltaic, and run-of-river hydro. This definition assumes these resources are fully utilized whenever available, and the remaining demand is supplied by dispatchable generation, energy storage, or imports.

Table~\ref{tab:exercise2-net-consumption} summarizes the statistics of gross consumption, must-run production, and net consumption for 2024.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Summary statistics of gross consumption, must-run renewable production, and net consumption, France 2024.}
  \label{tab:exercise2-net-consumption}
  \begin{tabular}{lccc}
    \toprule
    Quantity & Mean (GW) & Maximum (GW) & Minimum (GW) \\
    \midrule
    Gross consumption & 49.06 & 82.80 & 29.58 \\
    Must-run production & 11.55 & 37.51 & 0.08 \\
    Net consumption & 37.51 & 73.38 & -4.08 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The average net consumption is 37.51 GW, representing approximately 76.4\% of average gross consumption. The mean must-run production is 11.55 GW, or 23.5\% of average gross consumption. Notably, net consumption can become negative when renewable generation exceeds total demand. In 2024, the minimum net consumption reaches 4.08 GW below zero, indicating periods when renewable production alone would exceed demand. In a real system, such situations require demand management, exports, or curtailment of renewable generation.

Figure~\ref{fig:exercise2-net-consumption-ts} displays the hourly net consumption time series throughout 2024. The seasonal pattern remains visible, with higher average net demand during winter months and lower demand in summer. However, the variability is dampened compared to gross consumption because renewable production tends to contribute more during winter months when wind speeds are typically higher, partially offsetting the elevated heating demand.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/exercise_2-3.pdf}
  \caption{Hourly net electricity consumption in France, 2024. Net consumption represents the demand that must be supplied by controllable generation, storage, or imports after accounting for must-run renewable production.}
  \label{fig:exercise2-net-consumption-ts}
\end{figure}

A comparative view of gross and net consumption is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:exercise2-gross-net}. The difference between the two curves illustrates the magnitude of renewable production at each point in time. The decoupling is most apparent during autumn and winter, when wind generation is typically higher, while summer months show smaller differences due to reduced wind availability.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/exercise_2-3_gross_net.pdf}
  \caption{Gross and net electricity consumption in France, 2024. The vertical gap between the curves represents the must-run renewable production at each hour.}
  \label{fig:exercise2-gross-net}
\end{figure}

\subsection{Net load duration curve}

The load duration curve is a classical tool in power system planning that shows how many hours per year the system must supply a given load level or greater. It is obtained by sorting all hourly load values in decreasing order and plotting them against the corresponding number of hours. This representation is particularly useful for sizing generation capacity and understanding the frequency of different demand regimes.

Figure~\ref{fig:exercise2-ldc} compares the load duration curves of gross consumption and net consumption. The horizontal axis represents the cumulative number of hours per year for which the load equals or exceeds the value on the vertical axis.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.95\textwidth]{figures/exercise_2-4.pdf}
  \caption{Load duration curves for gross and net consumption, France 2024. The net load duration curve is consistently below the gross curve because variable renewable production reduces residual demand at all times.}
  \label{fig:exercise2-ldc}
\end{figure}

The net load duration curve lies entirely below the gross load duration curve, reflecting the reduction in residual demand provided by renewable generation. The average demand reduction is 11.55 GW, equivalent to 23.5\% of average gross consumption. However, the vertical separation between the two curves is not constant. During peak-load hours (left side of the curve), the gap is often smaller because peak demand typically occurs during winter evenings when wind generation may be significant but is not always guaranteed. Conversely, during low-load periods (right side of the curve), the gap can be substantial if renewable resources are particularly abundant.

The net load duration curve is the appropriate demand profile for sizing dispatchable generation, energy storage, and import capacity in a system where renewables are prioritized. The maximum net load reaches approximately 73 GW, while the minimum dips below zero, indicating the need for demand flexibility or export capacity to handle surplus renewable generation. The shape of the net load curve, showing a relatively flat middle section and steep rise toward peak loads, is typical of systems with significant variable renewable penetration and implies that backup capacity must be maintained to handle peak-demand situations.

\section{Exercise 3. Optimization of an adapted generation fleet}

\subsection{Economic assumptions and annualized investment cost}

The objective of this exercise is to size a simplified dispatchable generation fleet able to supply the net demand obtained in Exercise 2. The model considers four thermal technologies: nuclear, coal, combined-cycle gas turbine, and open-cycle oil turbine. The annualized investment cost is taken as 8\% of the investment cost. For a plant of \SI{1}{\mega\watt} electrical capacity, this gives the fixed annual costs shown in Table~\ref{tab:exercise3-investment}.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Investment assumptions and annualized fixed costs for \SI{1}{\mega\watt} of capacity.}
  \label{tab:exercise3-investment}
  \begin{tabular}{lcc}
    \toprule
    Technology & Capex (MEUR/MW) & Annualized investment (kEUR/MW/year) \\
    \midrule
    Nuclear & 6.0 & 480 \\
    Coal & 1.5 & 120 \\
    Gas CCGT & 1.0 & 80 \\
    Oil OCGT & 0.7 & 56 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

Nuclear power has the highest fixed cost because of its high capital intensity. Oil and gas turbines have much lower fixed costs, which makes them suitable candidates for infrequent operation despite their higher variable costs.

\subsection{Fuel and carbon costs}

The fuel cost per electrical megawatt-hour is obtained by dividing the thermal fuel cost by the electrical efficiency. The carbon cost is computed with a carbon price of \SI{80}{EUR/tCO2}. Table~\ref{tab:exercise3-variable-costs} summarizes the resulting variable costs.

\begin{table}[H]
  \centering
  \caption{Fuel, carbon, and total variable costs.}
  \label{tab:exercise3-variable-costs}
  \begin{tabular}{lcccc}
    \toprule
    Technology & Efficiency & Fuel cost & CO2 cost & Variable cost \\
    & & (EUR/MWh$_e$) & (EUR/MWh$_e$) & (EUR/MWh$_e$) \\
    \midrule
    Nuclear & 0.35 & 14.29 & 0.00 & 14.29 \\
    Coal & 0.40 & 25.00 & 80.00 & 105.00 \\
    Gas CCGT & 0.60 & 50.00 & 32.00 & 82.00 \\
    Oil OCGT & 0.35 & 142.86 & 48.00 & 190.86 \\
    \bottomrule
  \end{tabular}
\end{table}

The carbon price has a strong effect on coal. Although coal has a lower fuel cost than gas in thermal energy terms, its emissions make its variable cost higher than the gas combined-cycle plant. Nuclear has the lowest variable cost because the assumed direct CO2 intensity is zero and the fuel cost is low after conversion to electrical output.

\subsection{Production cost as a function of operating hours}

For \SI{1}{\mega\watt} of capacity, the annual cost of each technology is written as
\[
C(h) = C_{\mathrm{fixed}} + c_{\mathrm{var}} h,
\]
where \(h\) is the number of full-load operating hours during the year. Figure~\ref{fig:exercise3-screening} shows the resulting screening curves.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering
  \includegraphics[width=0.82\textwidth]{figures/exercise_3-4.pdf}
  \caption{Screening curves for the candidate dispatchable technologies.}
  \label{fig:exercise3-screening}
\end{figure}

The lower envelope of these curves gives the least-cost technology for each operating duration. Oil is competitive only for very short operating times because of its low fixed cost and high variable cost. Gas is least-cost for intermediate use. Nuclear becomes least-cost for long operating durations because its low variable cost offsets its high fixed cost. Coal is not on the least-cost envelope under the assumed carbon price.

\section{Exercise 4. Storage sizing}

\subsection{Statistical distribution of net demand}

The first step is to examine the daily structure of the residual demand. The net demand is the same as in Exercise 2 and Exercise 3, namely the gross consumption minus onshore wind, photovoltaic production, and run-of-river hydro production. For each hour of the day, the 2024 hourly values were grouped by local date. Since 2024 is a leap year, the data set contains 366 daily values for each hour rather than 365.

Figure~\ref{fig:exercise4-hourly-distribution} shows the resulting statistical distribution. The boxplots give the variability of the net demand at each hour of the day across the year.

\subsection{Daily storage operation}

The storage unit considered here has a maximum charging and discharging power of \SI{1}{\mega\watt} and an energy capacity of \SI{2}{\mega\watt\hour}. A simple daily operation rule was used. For each local day, the unit charges at \SI{1}{\mega\watt} during the two hours with the lowest net demand and discharges at \SI{1}{\mega\watt} during the two hours with the highest net demand. Losses are neglected, so the daily charged and discharged energies are both equal to \SI{2}{\mega\watt\hour}.

Figure~\ref{fig:exercise4-storage-week} illustrates this operation for the week from 8 to 15 January 2024. The change is small at the national scale because the storage unit is only \SI{1}{\mega\watt}, while the residual demand is measured in gigawatts. The economic calculation is therefore made per storage unit.

\begin{figure}[H]
  \centering

  \label{fig:exercise4-storage-week}
\end{figure}

The dispatchable fleet from Exercise 3 was used to value the storage operation. The production stack is nuclear, then combined-cycle gas, then open-cycle oil. The storage unit avoids production during high-demand hours and adds production during low-demand hours. The annual fuel saving is obtained by comparing the fuel cost of the dispatch before and after storage operation. Avoided emissions are computed in the same way from the CO2 intensities of the displaced generation.

\subsection{Electric vehicle charging case}

The final case considers the charging of 1000 electric vehicles. The maximum charging power is \SI{7}{\kilo\watt} per vehicle, giving a fleet charging power of \SI{7}{\mega\watt}. The annual distance and consumption assumptions are the same as in Exercise 1, namely \SI{11700}{\kilo\meter} per vehicle and \SI{18}{\kilo\watt\hour} per \SI{100}{\kilo\meter}. This gives \SI{2106}{\mega\watt\hour} per year for the fleet.

The charging is split into two sessions per week, one during the weekend and one on Wednesday or Thursday. With 104 sessions per year, the energy per session is approximately \SI{20.25}{\mega\watt\hour}. Each session is therefore represented by three hourly steps at a maximum of \SI{7}{\mega\watt}, with the last hour adjusted to match the required energy. Each session is placed in the consecutive hours with the lowest net demand within its allowed time window.

The renewable supply calculation uses the 2024 wind and photovoltaic production profiles normalized by the installed capacities. For each \SI{1}{\mega\watt} of installed capacity, the energy that can be supplied during the charging hours without exceeding the charging demand is computed. The most effective renewable source is installed first, then the second source is added under the same no-curtailment condition.
